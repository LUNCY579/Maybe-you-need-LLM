## 1. Pre-training

*How a model learns language before anyone asks it anything*

Self-supervised objectives turn raw text into a training signal. This module contrasts the three pre-training families — decoder-only, encoder-only and encoder–decoder — and works through masked language modelling as the canonical encoder objective.

*Printed pages:* pp. 1–35

**Learning outcomes**

- Explain why self-supervision replaced task-specific labelled data
- Compare next-token, masked-token and span-corruption objectives
- Read the masked language modelling objective and its loss
- Predict what a pre-training objective can and cannot produce

**Common misconceptions**

- ❌ Pre-training teaches the model to follow instructions.
  ✅ Pre-training produces a distribution over text and a set of internal representations. Instruction following, refusal behaviour and question answering are all imposed afterwards, by fine-tuning, preference learning or prompting.
- ❌ A masked language model can generate text, so it is a language model like the decoder.
  ✅ Masked prediction is an encoder objective. At inference there is no mask to fill, so the objective is out of distribution and the model cannot generate left to right.
- ❌ More parameters always make a better model.
  ✅ Past a certain point, parameters without more tokens buy very little. Data and parameters have to grow together, and the compute-optimal point moves as the data grows.

### Lesson 1. Self-supervised pre-training

*Why predicting text is enough to learn language*

*Estimated study time:* 22 minutes

**What you will learn**

- State the difference between unsupervised, supervised and self-supervised pre-training
- Explain why self-supervision scales where human annotation does not
- Identify what a pre-training objective optimises, and what it leaves unspecified

#### Concept Three ways to obtain supervision

**Pre-training** is the process of optimising a neural network on a general objective before anyone knows which task it will eventually perform. The book frames it as two separate concerns: first fit parameters $\theta$ on the general objective, then adapt the resulting model $\hat{g}(\cdot)$ to downstream tasks by **fine-tuning** or prompting.

What changed the field was not the idea of pre-training — that is decades old — but the source of the supervision signal:

- **Unsupervised pre-training.** Optimise a criterion that is not tied to any task at all, for example reconstructing inputs layer by layer. It helps optimisation and regularisation, but does not by itself teach language.
- **Supervised pre-training.** Train on a large labelled task such as sentiment classification, then swap the head and fine-tune. Effective, but it inherits the cost of labelled data.
- **Self-supervised pre-training.** Construct targets from the text itself: hide part of the input and predict it, or predict the next token. The supervision signal scales with the corpus rather than with human effort, which is why it produced foundation models.

#### Concept Why the objective matters more than the architecture

A **pre-training** objective defines what the model is rewarded for. Predict the next token and you build a generator; reconstruct masked tokens from both sides of context and you build an encoder that represents text well but cannot generate it fluently; corrupt spans and regenerate them and you build a sequence-to-sequence system. The same Transformer stack supports all three — the objective is what decides which capabilities emerge.

That choice also determines the kind of data the model can use. Next-token prediction consumes text left-to-right, so it needs no annotation and can process any corpus. **Masked-token prediction** needs only the text plus a masking procedure. The data requirements are therefore almost identical; the difference is in the product.

A precise statement of what pre-training does not do

Pre-training does not encode a task. It produces a distribution over text and a set of internal representations. Instruction following, refusal behaviour and question answering are all imposed later, by fine-tuning, preference learning or prompting.

Self-supervised targets from one sentence

Next-token prediction turns one sequence into m − 1 training examples without a single human label.

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{i} \log \Pr_\theta(x_i \mid x_0, \dots, x_{i-1})$$

| # | input (context) | target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | the | model |
| 2 | the model | learns |
| 3 | the model learns | from |
| 4 | the model learns from | text |

Sequence length 5 produced 4 training targets. In a real corpus this multiplication happens across trillions of tokens.

This is the mechanism that lets pre-training consume raw text. The &#x27;label&#x27; is simply the next token in the corpus.

Worked example

**How many training targets does one sentence carry?**

Take one short sentence: the model learns from the text — six words after tokenization with our demonstrative tokenizer. Next-token prediction creates one target per position that has a successor, so the count is:

$$\text{targets} = m - 1 = 6 - 1 = 5$$

Each target is a (context, next token) pair, so the loss has five terms. BERT-style masking at 15% selects $\lceil 0.15 \times 6 \rceil = 1$ position in a sentence this short, which is one of the reasons masking is used on longer documents: on short sequences the target density is very low.

The multiplication that matters is at corpus scale. A one-trillion-token corpus yields roughly one trillion next-token predictions, versus the thousands of examples a small labelled dataset provides.

**The ideas**

- Pre-training optimises a general objective on unlabelled text before any downstream task is known.
- Self-supervision derives labels from the data itself: hide part of the input, predict it back.
- The resulting model is adapted later by fine-tuning or prompting.

#### Practice

**Q. What makes a pre-training objective "self-supervised"? — concept**

- ✓ **Labels are derived from the input data itself, such as predicting a hidden part of the text**
- The model is trained without any loss function
- A separate teacher model supplies soft labels for every token
- Humans annotate every training example

> **Why:** Self-supervision constructs the supervision signal from the raw text: hide or shift part of the input and predict it. No human labels and no separate teacher are required.

**Q. In the book’s unified view o = g(x₀, x₁, …, xₘ; θ), what are the two aspects of pre-training? — concept**

- ✓ **Optimising θ on a general objective, then adapting ĝ(·) to downstream tasks**
- Compressing the dataset, then decompressing it at inference
- Training a tokenizer, then freezing it forever
- Maximising accuracy on one specific benchmark from the start

> **Why:** Pre-training first optimises θ on a task-agnostic objective, then the resulting model ĝ(·) is adapted by fine-tuning or prompting.

**Q. A team has 2,000 unlabelled documents and 200 labelled examples for a classification task. Why does self-supervised pre-training usually beat supervised pre-training here? — concept**

- ✓ **The unlabelled corpus can train a general representation, and the small labelled set only needs to adapt it**
- Unlabelled data always produces better accuracy than labelled data
- Supervised pre-training cannot use neural networks
- Self-supervised pre-training eliminates the need for a task head

> **Why:** The scarce resource is labels; self-supervision converts the abundant unlabelled corpus into a training signal and leaves the small labelled set for adaptation.

**Q. A company has fifty thousand support transcripts and no labels, and trains a model on them by predicting the next word. What has the model learned that a keyword search over the same transcripts would not have? — transfer**

- ✓ **The regularities that make a plausible continuation likely: which words follow which, in which contexts, and therefore what the transcripts are about**
- The transcripts verbatim, because the objective is to reproduce the input
- Only the vocabulary, because only the input tokens are supervised
- Nothing useful: without labels the objective is unsupervised by definition

> **Why:** Predicting the next word forces the model to model the regularities of the domain, because a continuation is only likely if the model knows what tends to follow what. A keyword search finds strings; the model learns which words follow which in which contexts, and that is what makes its continuation plausible. "Unsupervised" describes where the labels come from, not what can be learned from them.

### Lesson 2. Three pre-training objectives

*Decoder-only, encoder-only and encoder–decoder*

*Estimated study time:* 26 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Write the next-token (autoregressive) objective and read its loss
- Write the masked-language-modelling objective used by encoder-only models
- Explain span corruption in encoder–decoder pre-training
- Match a downstream task to the pre-training family that suits it

#### Concept Decoder-only: predict the next token

A decoder-only model factorises the probability of a sequence into per-token conditionals and trains by maximising their product:

$$\Pr(x_0, \dots, x_m) = \prod_{i} \Pr(x_i \mid x_0, \dots, x_{i-1})$$

Only previous tokens are visible, which is enforced by the **causal mask** discussed in Module 2. The important property is that training and inference perform the same operation: at generation time the model predicts one token given a prefix, exactly as it did during training.

#### Concept Encoder-only: reconstruct what was hidden

An encoder-only model corrupts the input and reconstructs the corrupted positions using the full surrounding context. The objective only counts masked positions, so the loss is sparse but each term is well conditioned.

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log \Pr_\theta\!\left(x_i \mid \tilde{x}\right)$$

Because the objective conditions on both directions, it produces strong representations for classification, extraction and ranking. It also means the model expects a corrupted input, which is why it does not generate text the way a decoder does.

#### Concept Encoder–decoder: reconstruct spans

**Span corruption** deletes contiguous chunks and asks the decoder to regenerate them, conditioned on the corrupted source. The corruption is less local than single-token masking and the target is a whole sequence, which makes the objective a genuine sequence-to-sequence task and suits translation, summarisation and question answering over long context.

Choosing a family is a product decision

If you need to generate text, start with a decoder. If you need a fixed-length representation for a classification or retrieval pipeline, an encoder is usually smaller and cheaper. If your task is naturally input→output with a differing output length, an encoder–decoder matches the shape.

**The ideas**

- Decoder-only: predict the next token from a causal prefix, so the model can generate.
- Encoder-only: reconstruct masked tokens using both sides of the context, so the model represents.
- Encoder–decoder: corrupt spans in the input, generate the removed content.

#### Practice

**Q. Which objective can be used directly for left-to-right generation at inference time? — concept**

- ✓ **Causal next-token prediction (decoder-only)**
- Masked language modelling (encoder-only)
- Span corruption (encoder–decoder)
- None of them; generation always needs a separate model

> **Why:** A decoder-only model is trained on exactly the operation used for generation: predict the next token given the preceding ones.

**Q. What does a causal mask do inside self-attention? — concept**

$$\operatorname{Mask}_{(i,k)} = 0 \;\text{if}\; i \ge k,\; -\infty \;\text{otherwise}$$

- ✓ **It sets the attention weight of future positions to zero so token i only sees i and earlier**
- It removes padding tokens from the batch
- It scales scores by 1/√d to keep gradients stable
- It replaces rare tokens with an unknown token

> **Why:** Adding −∞ before the softmax makes e^{−∞} = 0, so every future position receives exactly zero attention weight.

**Q. Why do encoder–decoder models such as T5 corrupt spans instead of single tokens? — concept**

- ✓ **Predicting a whole removed span creates a harder, more structured target and shortens the output sequence**
- Single-token masking is mathematically impossible in an encoder–decoder model
- Sparse corruption requires a separate tokenizer per span
- Span corruption removes the need for a decoder

> **Why:** Span corruption asks the decoder to reconstruct contiguous chunks, which is a sequence-to-sequence task and yields more supervision per removed region.

**Q. A team must both classify a document and write a summary of it. Which pre-training family fits, and why does the shape of the prediction decide it? — transfer**

- ✓ **An encoder–decoder: the encoder reads the whole document in both directions, the decoder writes the summary one token at a time**
- A decoder-only model, because it can be prompted to classify and then to summarise
- An encoder-only model, because classification is an encoder task and a summary is a classification with more classes
- Either one, because the objective does not constrain the architecture

> **Why:** The three families differ in the shape of the prediction, and the shape decides what the model can do at inference. A decoder has only ever continued text, so it cannot fill a blank in the middle; an encoder has only ever filled blanks, so it cannot continue. The encoder–decoder does both, with one set of weights.

### Lesson 3. BERT as a worked example

*Masked language modelling, then adaptation*

*Estimated study time:* 24 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Describe the BERT masking procedure: which tokens are replaced and how
- Explain the [CLS] and [SEP] conventions and how a sequence classifier is attached
- Explain why masked pre-training cannot generate text the way a decoder does
- Adapt a pre-trained encoder to a classification task

#### Concept The masking recipe

BERT selects roughly 15% of positions and, for each selected position, replaces the token with [MASK] 80% of the time, with a random vocabulary token 10% of the time, and leaves it unchanged 10% of the time. The loss is computed at all selected positions — the model must recover the original token in every case.

The mixture exists because [MASK] never appears in real input. If every selected position were masked, the encoder would learn to condition on a symbol that is absent at **fine-tuning** time. Leaving the true token or a random token in place keeps the model honest about unmasked input.

A frequently misread detail

15% is the fraction of positions *selected*, not the fraction replaced by [MASK] . In the book’s description only about 12% of tokens end up as [MASK] , because the other 20% of selections are handled differently.

#### Concept Special tokens and task heads

BERT prepends a special classification token [CLS] and separates sequences with [SEP] . After **pre-training** , the pooled representation of [CLS] — or a sequence of token representations, depending on the task — feeds a small task-specific head that is trained on labelled data.

This is the adaptation recipe in its simplest form: the encoder provides representations, the head provides the decision, and **fine-tuning** adjusts both slightly. For sentence classification the head is essentially a softmax layer over the pooled representation.

#### Concept What masked pre-training cannot do

A **masked language model** has never been asked to continue text. Asking it to do so means running it out of distribution, and the result is typically incoherent. Likewise, the model has no notion of an **autoregressive** generation loop, no **KV cache** and no decode phase.

This is a clean illustration of the general principle: the **pre-training** objective determines which downstream behaviours can be obtained cheaply. Anything outside the objective must be obtained by **fine-tuning** on the right data or by prompting a model that was trained for it.

**Q. In BERT-style masking, a chosen position is replaced by [MASK] with probability 0.8, a random token with 0.1, and left unchanged with 0.1. Why not always use [MASK]? — concept**

- ✓ **Because [MASK] never appears at fine-tuning time, the mixed recipe keeps the model usable on ordinary text**
- Because [MASK] is a reserved token that cannot be embedded
- Because random tokens increase the vocabulary size
- Because the loss cannot be computed when every target is [MASK]

> **Why:** The encoder must remain competent on unmasked input. Occasionally leaving the true token or a random token in place prevents the model from assuming that the input always contains [MASK].

**Q. You need a system that classifies sentences into 8 topics with only 5,000 labelled examples. Which plan fits the book’s guidance best? — concept**

- ✓ **Fine-tune a pre-trained encoder with a new classification head on the labelled data**
- Pre-train a decoder-only model from scratch on the 5,000 examples
- Use the masked language modelling objective as the final classifier
- Concatenate the labelled examples into one long prompt and hope for a label

> **Why:** This is the canonical adaptation recipe: reuse a pre-trained encoder and fine-tune a task head on the small labelled set.

**Q. A company adds twenty of its own product names to the vocabulary of a masked language model and continues training. Why are the new tokens badly predicted at first, and what would fix it? — transfer**

- ✓ **The embedding rows for the new tokens are still at their random initialisation, so the model has never seen them; continued training moves them into place**
- The [MASK] token was trained only on the original vocabulary, so it can never predict the new tokens
- The new tokens are rare in the corpus, so the model cannot learn them however long it trains
- The output head is tied to the input embeddings, so adding tokens breaks the tie

> **Why:** A new token starts as a random vector that the model has no reason to associate with anything, so it is predicted at chance until training moves it. The mechanism is otherwise unaffected: the model is still told which position to predict, and the output head is still a softmax over the whole — now larger — vocabulary.

**Common misconceptions**

- ❌ The causal mask is a filter applied after the softmax.
  ✅ It is added to the scores before the softmax, as minus infinity, so a masked position receives exactly zero weight rather than a small one.
- ❌ Dividing the scores by the square root of the dimension is only numerical tidiness.
  ✅ Without it the dot products are large enough that the softmax is effectively one-hot, the gradients vanish, and the model cannot learn to spread attention.
- ❌ A longer context costs more but changes nothing else.
  ✅ Attention is quadratic in the context length and the cache grows linearly, so ingesting a long prompt is super-linear in its length — and the extra tokens displace the instruction.

#### Practice

**Q. A sequence has length m and hidden dimension d. What are the shapes of Q, K and V? — concept**

- ✓ **All three are m × d**
- Q is m × m, K and V are d × d
- Q is m × d, K and V are d × m
- All three are d × d

> **Why:** Each position contributes one row, so Q, K, V ∈ ℝ^{m×d}; the score matrix Q Kᵀ is m × m.

**Q. Raw scores for one row are [2, 0] with head dimension d = 4 and no mask. Which row of attention weights is correct? — calculation**

$$\operatorname{Softmax}\!\left(\frac{q k^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)$$

- ✓ **[0.7311, 0.2689]**
- [0.88, 0.12]
- [0.5, 0.5]
- [1.0, 0.0]

> **Why:** Divide by √4 = 2 to get [1, 0]; then softmax([1,0]) = [e/(e+1), 1/(e+1)] ≈ [0.731, 0.269].

**Q. Why is the first row of a causally masked attention matrix always [1, 0, 0, …]? — concept**

- ✓ **Position 0 has no past, so the softmax is taken over a single legal position**
- The first token is always a special token with a fixed embedding
- Softmax always returns a one-hot vector for short rows
- Because the value matrix is lower triangular

> **Why:** With masking, row 0 can only attend to column 0, and a softmax over a single value is exactly 1.

**Q. What does adding a positional encoding to a token embedding actually provide? — concept**

- ✓ **Order information, since attention itself is permutation-invariant**
- A larger embedding dimension
- A probability distribution over the vocabulary
- A causal mask for the attention scores

> **Why:** Attention compares rows without any notion of order; positional signals inject the index so the model can distinguish “model learns” from “learns model”.

**Q. A model is trained without the causal mask. It reaches a near-zero training loss, and yet it produces nonsense when it generates. Explain both facts with one reason. — transfer**

- ✓ **Each position could attend to the future, so the model learned to copy the answer rather than to predict it; at generation time there is no future to copy**
- The loss was computed on the wrong split, so the model was never really trained
- The model overfitted, which is why the training loss is low and the generation is bad
- The softmax was applied to the wrong axis

> **Why:** Without the mask the answer is in the input, so the task is trivial and the loss says nothing about the model’s ability to predict. At generation, position i is asked for token i+1 with only the past available — a distribution the model never had to learn, because it never had to predict without seeing the answer.

### Lesson 5. Training at scale

*Data, parallelism and scaling laws*

*Estimated study time:* 28 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Describe how data is prepared and deduplicated before large-scale pre-training
- Explain data, tensor, pipeline and sequence parallelism at a conceptual level
- Interpret scaling-law trade-offs between parameters, data and compute

#### Concept Data before architecture

At scale, the pipeline matters as much as the model. Text is collected, filtered for quality, deduplicated, and mixed across domains. Deduplication is not housekeeping: repeated documents are memorised more easily, distort the effective distribution and inflate apparent loss progress.

A **scaling law** says how the loss of a **language model** falls as data and parameters grow. The useful question is not how big the model should be, but what keeps it busy: past a certain point, adding parameters without adding tokens buys very little.

Model modifications �?" architecture tweaks, better initialisation, mixed precision �?" are then combined with distributed training, where the interesting quantity is not device count but how often devices must synchronise relative to how much work they do between synchronisations.

#### Concept Parallelism in one paragraph each

- ****Data parallelism**** replicates the model on every device and splits the batch. Gradients are averaged across replicas. Memory per device stays high.
- ****Tensor parallelism**** splits individual weight matrices across devices so a single layer fits in aggregate memory. It requires frequent collective communication.
- **Pipeline parallelism** assigns different layers to different devices and streams micro-batches through the resulting stages, trading pipeline bubbles for memory relief.

#### Concept Scaling laws

Loss falls as a power law in model size, data volume and compute, with an irreducible floor. The practical consequence is a budgeting question: for a fixed compute budget approximately $C \approx 6ND$ (parameters times tokens), there is a best split between making the model bigger and training it longer.

$$L(N, D) \approx L_\infty + \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha} + \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\beta}$$

The exponents are fitted empirically, not derived, and they depend on architecture, data mixture and optimiser. They are useful for planning; they are not laws of nature.

### Lesson 6. Long sequences and the KV cache

*Why context is expensive, and how systems pay for it*

*Estimated study time:* 30 minutes

**What you will learn**

**The ideas**

- Attention scores cost O(T²·d) per head per layer; the cache costs O(L·h_kv·d·T).
- Caching keys and values turns per-token decode from quadratic recomputation into a linear read.
- Sharing key/value heads shrinks the cache at the price of less per-head expressiveness.

#### Practice

**Q. A model has 32 layers, 8 KV heads, head dimension 128, context 4,096 and stores fp16 (2 bytes). Roughly how large is one sequence’s KV cache? — calculation**

$$2 \times L \times h_{kv} \times d_h \times T \times \text{bytes}$$

- ✓ **≈ 512 MiB**
- ≈ 64 MiB
- ≈ 2 GiB
- ≈ 8 MiB

> **Why:** 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 4096 × 2 = 2^{29} bytes = 512 MiB. That is already half a gigabyte for a single 4k-token sequence.

**Q. Grouped-query attention reduces the KV cache by: — concept**

- ✓ **Sharing key/value heads among several query heads**
- Reducing the number of layers
- Quantising activations to 8 bits
- Skipping every other attention layer

> **Why:** GQA keeps many query heads but fewer key/value heads, so the cache term L·h_kv·d_h·T shrinks while query expressiveness stays higher than in multi-query attention.

**Q. Quadrupling the context length from 4k to 16k multiplies prefill attention work by about: — calculation**

$$\text{work} \propto n^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \left(\frac{16\text{k}}{4\text{k}}\right)^2 = 16$$

- ✓ **16**
- 4
- 8
- 2

> **Why:** Prefill attention is quadratic in the sequence length: (16k/4k)² = 16. Decode cost, which is linear in the cache length, only grows by 4.

**Q. A team has to read a hundred-thousand-token document once and then ask it several questions. Which costs are paid once and which are paid per question? — transfer**

- ✓ **The prefill — the quadratic attention over the whole document — is paid once; the cache is then reused, so each question costs a decode pass over the stored keys and values**
- Both are paid per question, because nothing is kept between them
- The prefill is paid per question and the cache is paid once
- Neither matters, because attention is linear

> **Why:** The quadratic term is paid when the tokens are read. The cache exists precisely so that the read is not paid again: each new token attends to the stored keys and values linearly. So a long document is expensive once and cheap to interrogate afterwards.

### Lesson 7. Prompt design and in-context learning

*Instructions, demonstrations and output contracts*

*Estimated study time:* 26 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Structure a prompt into instruction, context, examples and output contract
- Explain in-context learning as conditioning rather than weight updates
- Predict how adding demonstrations changes the model distribution
- Diagnose prompt failures such as ambiguity and conflicting constraints

#### Concept A prompt is a specification, not a wish

Everything a frozen model knows about your task at inference time is in the **context window** . A useful decomposition of that context is four parts: the **instruction** (what to do), the **context** (what to use), the **demonstrations** (what the answer should look like) and the **output contract** (what shape the answer must take).

The prompt is not the only handle on the model. A **foundation model** has already been pre-trained on a broad corpus, so a prompt is a request to use what is already there rather than a way to install new knowledge.

Each part competes for the same budget. The default prompt assembled in the lab uses 199 tokens of a 64-token window, of which 118 tokens are demonstrations and retrieved text. Prompt engineering, at its core, is allocation under a hard constraint.

$$\underbrace{x_{\text{sys}}}_{\text{instruction}} \; \underbrace{x_{\text{demos}}}_{\text{examples}} \; \underbrace{x_{\text{ctx}}}_{\text{retrieved}} \; \underbrace{x_{\text{q}}}_{\text{question}} \; \rightarrow \; y$$

#### Concept In-context learning is conditioning

No parameters change during inference. Demonstrations influence the output because they are part of the sequence the model conditions on: the conditional distribution of the next token shifts toward the pattern the examples establish. This is why a handful of examples can “teach” a format, and why the effect disappears the moment the examples fall out of the **context window** .

It also explains the failure modes. If the demonstrations are inconsistent with each other, the model interpolates. If the demonstration labels are wrong, the model faithfully reproduces the errors. If the task requires knowledge the model does not have, no number of examples will supply it.

Zero-shot, one-shot, few-shot

The naming describes how many demonstrations precede the question. More demonstrations usually help with format adherence and hurt when they consume the space needed for context.

#### Concept Diagnosing a bad prompt

Most prompt failures are specification failures, and they are diagnosable:

- **Ambiguity** — two instructions demand incompatible outputs (“one word” and “explain in detail”). The model must guess which to satisfy.
- **Missing contract** — no format is specified, so the answer is a paragraph when a value was needed.
- **Context exhaustion** — the actual question is pushed out of the window by examples and retrieved text.
- **Unstated audience** — “summarise this” means something different to a researcher and an executive.

### Lesson 8. Chain of thought and decomposition

*Making intermediate steps explicit*

*Estimated study time:* 28 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Explain why writing intermediate steps changes what a single forward pass can compute
- Compare chain-of-thought prompting with problem decomposition
- Describe self-refinement and when it can hurt
- Design a prompt that forces a verifiable intermediate representation

#### Concept Computation per token is fixed

A decoder performs a fixed number of operations per generated token. If a problem needs more sequential computation than one forward pass provides, asking the model to produce intermediate steps gives it more computation: every generated token is another pass through the network, conditioned on everything written so far.

This is the mechanism behind **chain-of-thought** prompting. It is not that the model “needs to see its own reasoning”; it is that writing steps converts one hard computation into a sequence of easier ones, each able to use the previous result.

A testable prediction

If chain of thought works by adding computation, then restricting the trace length should hurt on tasks that need multi-step computation, and a model with more layers per token should need shorter traces for the same task. Both predictions have been empirically supported.

#### Concept Decomposition and self-refinement

Decomposition splits one hard step into several easier, individually checkable steps. **Self-refinement** adds a revision pass. The crucial difference between them and “think harder” is verifiability: a decomposed step can be checked by a calculation, a unit test or a retrieval lookup, whereas a vague instruction cannot.

Self-refinement without an external signal is mostly re-sampling. If the second attempt uses the same context and the same weights, it draws from the same distribution; when it changes the answer, it is not necessarily improving it.

#### Concept Search, verification and the cost of thinking

Once steps are explicit, they can be searched. Generating several candidate paths and selecting with a **verifier** is search with a learned heuristic; keeping the best $b$ partial solutions alive is **beam search** . The accounting is what matters: a strategy is only better if it buys accuracy at an acceptable number of verifier calls and generated tokens.

$$\text{accuracy} = \Pr\!\left(\exists\, \hat{y} \in \text{candidates}(C) : \text{verify}(\hat{y}) = \text{pass}\right)$$

The same tree scored with a step verifier versus only the final answer.

- step verifier Scores every partial solution, so search can prune early. Cost: a reliable step-level scorer, which is itself a trained model or a human process.

### Lesson 9. Retrieval, tools and learned prompts

*Giving the model facts and letting it call out*

*Estimated study time:* 30 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Describe retrieval-augmented generation as a prompt-assembly pipeline
- Score documents by cosine similarity in an explicit vector space
- Explain tool use as a structured action the model can emit
- Distinguish discrete prompt design from soft-prompt optimisation

#### Concept Retrieval converts an open-book question into a closed-book one

A model can only use information in its parameters or its context. Retrieval bridges the gap at run time: rank a document collection against the query, place the best passages into the prompt, and let the generator condition on them. The pipeline is prompt assembly with a search step in front.

The quality of the result depends on both halves. A generator that ignores retrieved text produces the same answer as before; a retriever that returns irrelevant passages actively hurts, because the **context window** is finite and plausible-looking noise displaces useful information.

#### Concept Similarity search is arithmetic

Documents and queries are projected into the same vector space, and ranking is a **cosine similarity** — the dot product normalised by both magnitudes.

$$\cos(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\lVert u \rVert \, \lVert v \rVert}$$

In the lab, documents are described by a hand-written six-axis keyword vector. That makes the mechanism inspectable, and it makes a limit explicit: the retrieval can only be as good as the vectors. Production systems learn those vectors, which is why retrieval quality is an embedding-model question, not just a nearest-neighbour question.

The honest framing of the toy retriever

Nothing here is a neural embedding model. The query vector is a lexical projection onto axis names, and the document vectors are authored by hand. The cosine similarities are exact for that representation, and the ranking behaviour — which passage wins and why — is faithful to how a real retriever is scored.

#### Concept Tools and learned prompts

**Tool use** extends the same idea: instead of text, the model emits a structured call, and the result returns as context. The contract matters more than the wording — a tool call is a small program, and malformed arguments fail regardless of how fluent the model is.

Prompt optimisation occupies a different regime. A **soft prompt** is a learned continuous vector prepended to the input; it is a parameter, not text. Discrete prompt search, by contrast, keeps the prompt readable and trades compute for quality. The two approaches differ in interpretability and in how the artefact is stored.

### Lesson 10. Instruction alignment with SFT

*Learning from demonstrations*

*Estimated study time:* 26 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Write the supervised fine-tuning loss on response tokens
- Explain why instruction data is constructed rather than collected naturally
- Describe data-quality filters and diversity requirements

#### Concept Alignment is a pipeline, not a single step

A pre-trained model predicts text. It does not follow instructions, does not decline harmful requests and has no notion of which of two plausible answers a person would prefer. Alignment is the set of techniques that add those behaviours, and it proceeds in stages: instruction alignment first, then preference alignment.

Instruction alignment means **supervised fine-tuning** on demonstrations: given an instruction and a good response, increase the probability of the response. Preference alignment means optimising which of several plausible responses is chosen, which requires a different kind of data.

#### Concept The SFT objective

**Supervised fine-tuning** maximises the likelihood of the demonstrated response token by token, given the instruction. In practice only response tokens contribute to the loss; the instruction is context.

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t} \log \Pr_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{**cross-entropy** used in **pre-training** , applied to a much smaller and much more carefully constructed dataset. The difference is entirely in the data: instructions and responses instead of raw documents.

#### Concept Data construction is the hard part

Instruction datasets are built from human writers, from existing supervised datasets reformatted as instructions, or from model-generated responses that pass quality filters. Three properties matter more than volume: coverage of the task types you care about, consistency of format, and honesty of the responses.

Style artefacts propagate. If 90% of demonstrations open with the same phrase, the fine-tuned model will over-produce it, because it is faithfully modelling the data distribution. That is a data bug with a modelling symptom, and no optimiser setting will fix it.

The limit of imitation

SFT can only push probability toward responses it has seen. If a better answer was never demonstrated, nothing in the objective prefers it. Preference learning exists precisely to escape this ceiling.

### Lesson 11. RLHF: reward models and policy training

*Learning from comparisons instead of demonstrations*

*Estimated study time:* 34 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Write the Bradley–Terry preference probability for a pair of responses
- Explain how a reward model is trained on comparisons
- Describe the RLHF objective: expected reward minus a KL penalty
- Explain why the KL penalty is necessary and what it protects

#### Concept Comparisons carry different information than demonstrations

Asking someone to write a good answer is expensive and produces one sample. Asking someone to rank two answers is cheap and produces a constraint. Preference data is therefore easier to collect at scale, and it encodes something demonstrations cannot: which of two plausible answers is better.

The **Bradley–Terry** model turns a comparison into a probability, assuming each response has a latent score and the chance of preferring one is a logistic function of the score difference:

$$\Pr(y_a \succ y_b \mid x) = \sigma\!\left(r(x, y_a) - r(x, y_b)\right)$$

#### Concept Training the reward model

Train a scorer so that observed preferences become likely: for every pair, increase the score difference between the chosen and the rejected response. This is logistic regression on differences, with no policy involved.

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_a, y_b)} \log \sigma\!\left(r_\phi(x, y_a) - r_\phi(x, y_b)\right)$$

The **reward model** is a learned proxy for human judgement, and every proxy can be exploited. Optimise it hard enough and the policy finds inputs that score highly without being useful — reward hacking.

#### Concept Policy optimisation with a leash

**RLHF** then tunes the **language model** to score well under the **reward model** , with a penalty for drifting away from the reference policy:

$$\tilde{\theta} = \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ -r(x,y) + \lambda \left( \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \right) \right]$$

The penalty is not decoration. Without it the policy is free to leave the region where the reward model is accurate, and it will: the reward model is only trustworthy near the data it was trained on.

What RLHF costs

Two models (policy and reward), a value function in the PPO variant, online sampling at every update, and a reward model that must be retrained when the policy distribution shifts. DPO was introduced to remove most of that machinery.

### Lesson 12. Direct preference optimisation

*Preference learning without a reward model*

*Estimated study time:* 34 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Reproduce the steps that turn a KL-regularised reward objective into a policy ratio
- Write the DPO preference probability using policy and reference log-ratios
- Explain why the partition function cancels inside a preference pair
- Compare DPO with RLHF in compute, data reuse and failure modes

#### Derivation Step 1 — the KL-regularised objective has a closed form

Start from the **RLHF** objective and treat the **reward model** and the reference policy as fixed. Rearranging the two log-probability terms lets them be written as a single log of a ratio:

$$\tilde{\theta} = \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x,y} \left[ \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log\!\left( \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda} r(x,y)\right) \right) \right]$$

The second term is an unnormalised distribution in $y$ . Normalising it defines a distribution $\pi^*$ , and the objective becomes a **KL divergence** between the policy and that distribution:

$$\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda} r(x,y)\right)$$

#### Derivation Step 2 — invert the relation to get the reward

The optimum is attained when the two distributions coincide, and inverting that equation expresses the reward through the policy’s **log-ratio** plus a normalising term:

$$r(x,y) = \lambda \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \lambda \log Z(x)$$

This is the pivot of the whole method. It says a **reward model** is not needed to represent reward — the policy itself implies one.

#### Derivation Step 3 — substitute into Bradley–Terry, and Z cancels

For a single prompt, both responses carry the same $\lambda \log Z(x)$ term, so subtracting one reward from the other eliminates it. Nothing is approximated: the cancellation is exact.

$$\Pr(y_a \succ y_b \mid x) = \sigma\!\left( \lambda \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_a \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_a \mid x)} - \lambda \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_b \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_b \mid x)} \right)$$

In the course’s toy policy, where $\log \pi$ is affine in the weights, the whole expression collapses to $\lambda (w - w_{\text{ref}}) \cdot \Delta\phi$ — which is what the lab computes.

#### Concept What changes in practice

The loss is now a supervised objective over a fixed preference dataset:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_a,y_b) \sim \mathcal{D}_r} \log \Pr(y_a \succ y_b \mid x)$$

Three consequences follow. No **reward model** is trained. No online sampling loop is required, so the dataset can be reused for multiple epochs. And the **reference model** must be kept available, because its log-probabilities appear in every pair’s term.

The trade-off, stated fairly

DPO is simpler and more sample-efficient than PPO-style RLHF, but the derivation assumes a fixed reward model and a preference model that matches the data. When the reward is genuinely non-stationary or when online exploration matters, the assumptions are the weak point — not the algebra.

## 5. Inference

*Prefill, decode and the arithmetic of serving tokens*

Serving a language model is two different workloads: a parallel prefill pass and a sequential decode loop. This module covers decoding algorithms, the KV cache, batching and parallelism, and how extra inference-time computation buys accuracy.

**Common misconceptions**

- ❌ The KV cache is a speed optimisation that changes nothing about the output.
  ✅ It is an exact reuse of the keys and values of earlier positions. It changes the cost of the computation, not its result.
- ❌ Prefill and decode are two stages of the same work.
  ✅ Prefill is one parallel pass over the prompt with quadratic attention. Decode is one token at a time against the cache, with linear attention per token.
- ❌ A lower temperature makes the model more accurate.
  ✅ It makes the output more concentrated. The entropy falls and the mode becomes more likely, but nothing about what the model knows has changed.

### Lesson 14. Decoding algorithms

*Greedy, beam, top-k, top-p and temperature*

*Estimated study time:* 30 minutes

**What you will learn**

#### Concept Decoding is a policy on top of the model

The model produces logits; the decoder decides how to turn them into a choice. **Greedy decoding** takes the argmax, **beam search** keeps several partial sequences, and sampling draws from a modified distribution. None of these change the model — they change which of its plausible continuations you see.

$$\Pr(y_i \mid x, y_{$\tau$ rescales the logits before filtering, so it interacts with **top-k** and **top-p** rather than acting independently. A small $\tau$ concentrates the mass; a large one flattens it.

#### Concept Two ways to truncate the tail

****Top-k**** keeps a fixed number of candidates. It is predictable and simple, but the right $k$ depends on the shape of the distribution: too small and the output is repetitive, too large and implausible tokens re-enter.

****Top-p**** (nucleus) keeps the smallest set whose cumulative probability reaches $p$ . When the distribution is peaked, that set is small; when it is flat, the set grows. The candidate count therefore adapts at every step.

$$V_i = \left\{ y_i^{\text{top}1}, \dots, y_i^{\text{top}k_p} \right\}, \qquad \sum_{j \le k_p} \Pr(y_i^{\text{top}j}) \ge p$$

In both cases the surviving probabilities are renormalised so they sum to one over the pool — otherwise the sampled distribution would be defective.

Greedy and beam, fairly

Greedy is reproducible and never wanders, which is what you want for structured output. Beam search explores several hypotheses but tends to favour short, high-probability sequences unless you calibrate length, and it spends $b$ times the compute per step.

#### Concept Determinism and reproducibility

Sampling is only reproducible if the random stream is. Seeding the generator makes a run replayable: the same configuration produces the same tokens, which is what allows a bug report to be reproduced and an experiment to be compared against a baseline.

Two independent streams from the same seed produce identical sequences — the lab shows both, side by side, to make that concrete.

**Common misconceptions**

- ❌ More reasoning tokens mean the model reasoned more carefully.

### Lesson 16. Reasoning as searched computation

*Prompting, sampling paths and verification*

*Estimated study time:* 30 minutes

**What you will learn**

- Compare independent sampling with search over a step tree
- Explain the role of a verifier and what happens when it is unreliable
- Estimate the accuracy/cost frontier of a reasoning strategy

#### Concept An operational definition of reasoning

“Does it reason?” is a question about an unobservable process. The useful version is operational: reasoning is the production of intermediate steps that a criterion can evaluate. That definition is testable, and it is the one used throughout this module.

Under it, three things become measurable: whether a step follows from its predecessors, whether the final answer is right, and how much computation was spent to get there. Every claim in the rest of the module is a statement about one of those three.

#### Concept Why more tokens can mean more thinking

A decoder performs a fixed amount of computation per generated token. A hard problem that cannot be solved in one pass can sometimes be solved by writing intermediate results: each token is another pass through the network, conditioned on everything written so far. This is the mechanism behind inference-time compute scaling, and it explains why a shorter trace on the same model is not equivalent to a longer one.

It also sets the cost model. More steps means more tokens, which means more time and memory. Extra computation is a trade, not a free improvement.

#### Concept Search over a step tree

Once steps are explicit, a solution is a path through a tree of partial states. Three strategies cover most practice:

- **Greedy** — expand the single best-scoring node. Cheapest; cannot recover from a locally attractive mistake.
- ****Beam search**** — keep the best $b$ partial solutions alive. Marginally more expensive per step, much more robust to a misleading first step.
- **Best-of-n sampling** — draw complete candidate paths independently and pick the best by **verifier** score. Simple to parallelise, but its accuracy depends on how often the sampling distribution produces a correct path at all.

The verifier is the bottleneck

Search optimises the objective it is given. A verifier that rewards confident wrong steps steers search toward wrong answers with high confidence — scaling the search makes the failure more reliable, not less. “Verification” is a promise about a checker, not about truth.

#### Concept Verification in practice

Verifiable rewards come from checkers: a unit test, a solver, a formal proof assistant, a database query whose result can be compared with the expected answer. Where a checker exists, reinforcement learning on it is well-posed. Where it does not, the reward must come from a learned model, and the reward-model caveats from Module 4 return in full.

- It eliminates the need for a verifier
- It cannot be combined with reinforcement learning

> **Why:** Step-level rewards localise credit, at the price of obtaining reliable step labels — the classic trade-off against outcome-only rewards.

**Q. Why does outcome-only supervision make credit assignment hard? — concept**

- ✓ **A single final reward must be attributed across many intermediate steps, and one bad step may be invisible**
- Because the final answer is unknown during training
- Because the model has no output layer
- Because tokenisation loses step boundaries

> **Why:** One scalar at the end gives no information about which step was responsible, especially when a wrong step still happens to produce a correct answer.

**Q. A team can afford process labels for one trace in ten, and outcome labels for all of them. What should it label with what, and why? — transfer**

- ✓ **Process-label the tenth, because the outcome labels already give the cheap signal and only the process labels can tell a right answer reached by a wrong route from one reached by a right one**
- Outcome-label the tenth and process-label the rest, because process labels are more informative per label
- Label everything with outcome labels; process labels are not worth their cost
- Label everything with process labels, because outcome labels are misleading

> **Why:** Outcome labels are cheap and give the broad signal; process labels are expensive and give the *specific* one. The combination that pays is to spend the expensive labels on a sample in order to learn what a good step looks like, and then use that to check the cheap ones. Process supervision does not make outcome labels wrong — it makes them blind.

#### Practice

**Q. A benchmark reports 82% accuracy, but 30% of its items appeared in the model’s pre-training data. What is the correct interpretation? — concept**

- ✓ **The score is inflated by contamination and may reflect memorisation rather than the claimed capability**
- The score is still valid because the metric is defined correctly
- The score is understated
- Contamination only matters for vision benchmarks

> **Why:** A metric measures a task setup. If the setup leaks training items, the measurement no longer supports the capability claim.

**Q. Which evaluation design best supports a claim that a method improves reasoning? — concept**

- ✓ **Held-out, contamination-checked tasks with a matched compute budget and an error analysis of the traces**
- A single benchmark run with the highest temperature
- Reporting only the best of ten runs
- Reporting the metric without the protocol

> **Why:** Matching compute makes the comparison about method rather than budget, and held-out contamination-checked tasks keep the claim about capability rather than recall.

**Q. A benchmark is published on the web, and a model is evaluated on it two years later. What has to be reported for the number to mean anything? — transfer**

- ✓ **The contamination rate and the item-level breakdown, because a model that has seen the answers scores higher for reasons that have nothing to do with reasoning**
- The overall score and the model size, because the score is the score
- The training data size, because a bigger model is better
- Nothing extra, because the benchmark is the benchmark

> **Why:** Anything published publicly before the evaluation is very likely in the pre-training or fine-tuning data. The number therefore measures a mixture of capability and recognition, and the two can only be separated by reporting how much of the benchmark was in the training data and how the model did item by item.

## Appendix A — Glossary

**Pre-training** — Training a general model before any task is specified

Optimising a model on a broad objective over large unlabelled corpora so that its parameters transfer to many downstream tasks. In the book’s unified notation the model o = g(x₀…xₘ; θ) is first fitted on the general objective, then adapted.

**Self-supervision** — Supervision constructed from the data itself

Building training targets by hiding or shifting parts of the input, so that no human annotation is needed. Masked-token prediction and next-token prediction are the two canonical instances.

**Foundation model** — A pre-trained model adapted to many tasks

A large pre-trained model that serves as a starting point for fine-tuning or prompting across many tasks, rather than being built for one task.

**Fine-tuning** — Continuing training on task or instruction data

Updating the parameters of a pre-trained model on a smaller, targeted dataset (labelled examples or instructions) so that it specialises.

**Autoregressive** — Each token conditioned on the ones before it

A factorisation of a sequence distribution into per-token conditionals: Pr(x) = ∏ᵢ Pr(xᵢ | x₀…xᵢ₋₁). This is the objective of decoder-only language models and the loop followed at generation time.

**Language model** — A distribution over token sequences

A model that assigns probabilities to sequences of tokens, or equivalently predicts the distribution of the next token given a context.

**Masked language model** — Predict hidden tokens from both sides

An encoder-only pre-training objective: replace a fraction of tokens with [MASK] (or a random token, or nothing), then predict the originals using the full surrounding context. Because inference at generation time never sees [MASK], the objective suits representation learning rather than open-ended generation.

**Span corruption** — Delete spans; ask the decoder to restore them

An encoder–decoder pre-training objective that removes contiguous spans from the input and trains the decoder to regenerate them, producing a sequence-to-sequence reconstruction task.

**Classification head** — A small output layer added for a task

A task-specific layer attached to a pre-trained encoder (often a softmax over pooled or [CLS] representations) and trained on labelled data.

**Token** — The unit a model reads and predicts

The atomic unit of text in a model’s vocabulary. Tokens may be whole words, subword pieces, characters or bytes; every sequence the model processes is a sequence of token ids.

**Token embedding** — A learned vector for a token id

A lookup table mapping each vocabulary item to a dense vector of dimension d. In this course the table is a deterministic toy table so that learners can inspect the numbers.

**Positional encoding** — Information about where a token sits

A vector added to the token embedding so the model can use order, since attention itself is permutation-invariant. Sinusoidal encodings use sin/cos at geometrically spaced frequencies; learned and rotary variants also exist.

**Query, key, value (Q, K, V)** — Three projections of the hidden states

Q, K and V are linear projections of the same hidden matrix H: Q = H W_q, K = H W_k, V = H W_v. Queries are compared with keys to produce attention weights, which are then applied to values.

**Scaled dot-product attention** — Softmax(QKᵀ/√d)·V

The core attention operation. The 1/√d scaling keeps logits in a range where the softmax has usable gradients as the head dimension grows.

**Causal mask** — Block attention to future positions

An additive matrix M with M(i,k) = 0 for i ≥ k and M(i,k) = −∞ otherwise, added to the scaled scores before the softmax. It makes the model autoregressive: position i can only see positions 0…i.

**Softmax** — Turn scores into a probability distribution

A normalising function that exponentiates scores and divides by their sum. Implementations subtract the maximum for numerical stability; the result is unchanged.

**Logits** — Unnormalised scores before softmax

The raw real-valued outputs of the final projection. Their softmax is the model’s token distribution; decoding algorithms operate on logits before or after this normalisation.

**Scaling law** — Predictable power-law loss trends

An empirical regularity: loss falls as a power law in model size, dataset size and compute, which allows training runs to be planned before they are executed.

**Compute-optimal scaling** — Balance parameters and tokens for a budget

The finding that, for a fixed compute budget, model size and training tokens should be increased together in a balanced ratio rather than scaling only one of them.

**Data parallelism** — Replicate the model, split the batch

Each device holds a copy of the parameters and processes a different batch slice; gradients are synchronised across devices. Memory per device stays high because every replica holds the whole model.

**Tensor parallelism** — Split a layer across devices

Individual weight matrices are partitioned across devices and partial results are combined with communication, which reduces per-device memory at the cost of frequent collective operations.

**KV cache** — Stored keys and values for past tokens

Autoregressive decoding needs the keys and values of every previous position. Storing them turns repeated recomputation into a memory read. The footprint is 2·L·h_kv·d_h·T·bytes per sequence.

**Grouped-query attention** — Fewer KV heads than query heads

Several query heads share one key/value head, reducing the cache by the ratio h_kv/h_q while retaining more expressiveness than multi-query attention (a single shared head).

**Context window** — How many tokens the model can condition on

The maximum number of tokens available in one forward pass. Prompts, retrieved passages, examples and generated tokens all consume this budget.

**Position interpolation** — Stretching a model beyond its trained length

Techniques that rescale or adjust positional information so that a model trained on shorter sequences can process longer contexts with acceptable quality.

**Prompt** — The text that conditions a frozen model

The input context given to a model at inference time: instructions, data, demonstrations and an output contract. Prompting changes conditioning, not parameters.

**In-context learning** — Learning a pattern from examples inside the prompt

The ability to infer a task from demonstrations in the context window, without parameter updates. It is conditioning: the demonstrations bias the next-token distribution.

**Zero-shot prompting** — Task description with no examples

A prompt that specifies the task in words without worked examples.

**Few-shot prompting** — A handful of demonstrations in the prompt

A prompt that includes several input→output examples before the real query. Examples cost context budget and can introduce their own biases.

**Chain of thought** — Write the intermediate steps

Prompting a model to produce reasoning steps before its answer. Because each token is a sequential computation step, longer traces let the model compute more before committing.

**Problem decomposition** — Split one hard task into easier sub-tasks

Rewriting a task as a sequence of sub-problems, each simple enough to be solved and checked individually.

**Self-refinement** — Revise a candidate answer

Producing a draft and then criticising or revising it. It only reliably helps when an external check supplies new information.

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** — Retrieve passages, then generate

A pipeline that selects relevant documents with a retriever and places them in the prompt so the generator conditions on up-to-date or private information.

**Cosine similarity** — Angle between two vectors

The dot product of two vectors divided by the product of their norms. Values near 1 mean aligned directions, which is how a retriever ranks passages against a query.

**Tool use** — The model emits a structured action

The model produces a call to an external function or API in a defined format; the result is returned to the context. Correctness depends on the contract, not only on fluent text.

**Soft prompt** — Learned continuous prompt vectors

Optimised input embeddings prepended to the input instead of discrete text. They are parameters, trained with gradients, and cannot be read as natural language.

**Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** — Imitate demonstrated responses

Fine-tuning on instruction/response pairs with the cross-entropy loss applied to response tokens, teaching the format and style of useful answers.

**Instruction tuning** — Fine-tuning to follow instructions

Supervised fine-tuning with instruction-formatted data, which turns a text continuation model into one that responds to requests.

**Cross-entropy loss** — Negative log-likelihood of the target

For a target distribution p and prediction q, H(p,q) = −Σ p log q. With a one-hot target it reduces to the negative log-probability of the correct class or token.

**RLHF** — Reinforcement learning from human feedback

A pipeline that trains a reward model on human comparisons, then optimises the policy to score well under that reward while staying close to a reference model.

**Reward model** — A scalar scorer trained on comparisons

A model r(x, y) that assigns a scalar quality score to a response. It is trained on pairwise preferences and then used as the objective for policy optimisation.

**Bradley–Terry model** — Preference as a sigmoid of score differences

A model of pairwise comparison where the probability that a beats b is the logistic function of the difference of their scores.

**KL divergence** — How far one distribution is from another

D_KL(p‖q) = Σ p log(p/q) ≥ 0, equal to zero only when the distributions match. It appears in RLHF as a penalty keeping the tuned policy close to its reference.

**PPO** — The policy-gradient algorithm used in RLHF

Proximal policy optimisation: a policy-gradient method that limits how far each update moves the policy, requiring both a policy and a value function.

**DPO (direct preference optimisation)** — Preference learning without an explicit reward model

Substituting the closed-form optimal policy into the Bradley–Terry likelihood yields a loss over policy/reference log-ratios, so preferences are optimised directly.

**Reference model** — The frozen model a policy is compared against

A copy of the model before preference tuning. Its log-probabilities appear in the DPO ratio and in the RLHF KL penalty, and it is never updated.

**Log-ratio** — log(π/π_ref) between policy and reference

The quantity log Pr_θ(y|x) − log Pr_ref(y|x). Under the DPO derivation it equals the reward divided by β, up to a prompt-dependent constant.

**Prefill** — Process the prompt in parallel

The first inference phase, in which all prompt positions are processed simultaneously and their keys and values are written into the cache.

**Decode** — Generate one token at a time

The second inference phase: each step computes one query, attends to cached keys and values, and appends a new token.

**Prefix caching** — Reuse a shared prompt’s cached state

Storing the KV state of a common prefix (a system prompt, for example) so that later requests skip its prefill computation.

**Continuous batching** — Admit and retire requests every step

Scheduling that adds new requests to a running batch as soon as slots free up, rather than waiting for a fixed batch to finish, which raises utilisation.

**Temperature (τ)** — Rescale logits before softmax

Dividing logits by τ before the softmax. τ < 1 sharpens the distribution (less randomness), τ > 1 flattens it (more). τ must be positive; zero is the limit, approached with a small positive value.

**Top-k sampling** — Sample from the k most likely tokens

Keep the k highest-probability tokens, renormalise their mass and sample from that pool. A fixed pool size can be too small in flat distributions and too large in peaked ones.

**Top-p (nucleus) sampling** — Sample from the smallest set reaching mass p

Sort tokens by probability, add them until the cumulative mass reaches p, then renormalise and sample. The pool size adapts to how peaked the distribution is.

**Greedy decoding** — Always take the most likely token

A deterministic decoder that selects argmax at every step. It is reproducible and predictable but cannot explore alternatives.

**Beam search** — Keep the best b partial sequences

A search that expands several candidate sequences in parallel and keeps the highest-scoring b at each step. Without a length or score calibration it favours short, high-probability outputs.

**Inference-time scaling** — Buy accuracy with extra computation at run time

Spending additional tokens, samples or search steps during inference — context scaling, search scaling, output ensembling, thinking-path generation — instead of changing the model.

**Throughput** — Tokens served per unit time

Aggregate production rate across all concurrent requests, as opposed to latency for one request. Batching raises throughput while usually increasing per-request latency.

**Reasoning** — Checkable intermediate computation

In this course, reasoning is defined operationally: producing intermediate steps that a criterion — a test, a verifier, a formal check — can evaluate. This keeps claims empirically testable.

**Test-time compute** — Computation spent after training

Any inference-time expenditure — longer traces, multiple samples, search — that can improve accuracy without changing weights.

**Verifier** — A scorer that judges candidate solutions

A function or model that scores a candidate answer or step. Search and best-of-n are only as good as the verifier they optimise against.

**Search strategy** — How candidate reasoning paths are explored

The rule for expanding and pruning partial solutions: greedy, beam, best-of-n, tree search. Each trades verifier calls against the risk of missing a correct path.

**Process supervision** — Reward every step, not only the answer

Training with step-level feedback, which localises credit assignment but requires reliable step labels and can be exploited by the policy.

**Process reward model (PRM)** — A scorer for intermediate steps

A reward model trained to score partial solutions, used to guide search or as a reward signal during training.

**Credit assignment** — Which step deserves the reward?

The problem of attributing a final outcome to the intermediate decisions that produced it — the central difficulty of outcome-only supervision.

**On-policy distillation** — Learn from correct traces the model itself generates

Training on reasoning traces sampled from the current model and filtered for correctness, which keeps the training distribution close to the inference distribution.

**Verifiable-reward training** — Rewards computed by a checker

Reinforcement learning where the reward comes from an automatic checker (a unit test, a solver, a proof checker) rather than a learned preference model.

**Benchmark** — A task suite used for evaluation

A fixed set of tasks with a scoring protocol. Benchmarks measure performance under a setup; they do not directly measure an abstract capability.

**Contamination** — Test data seen during training

Overlap between evaluation items and training data, which inflates scores and can convert a reasoning measurement into a recall measurement.

**pass@k** — Solve at least one of k attempts

A metric that counts a task as solved if any of k sampled attempts is correct. It measures coverage under a sampling budget, and should be reported with that budget.

**Outcome-based evaluation** — Judge the final answer only

Scoring a solution by its final answer. Cheap to automate and reproducible, but blind to whether the reasoning was sound or accidentally correct.
